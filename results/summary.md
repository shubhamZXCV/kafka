## Findings

**E2: the rebalance adds a heartbeat interval, not a few hundred milliseconds.** The
rebalance time splits into detection (5.26 ± 0.45 s) and a barrier (1.49 ± 0.47 s).
Detection is the session timeout minus the time since the dead consumer's last heartbeat.
At the barrier, the survivors learn of the rebalance only at their next heartbeat, up to
`heartbeat.interval.ms` (2 s) later, and must all rejoin before it completes. The SRS
bound leaves out that second part. It holds only when the survivors' heartbeats happen to
fall soon after the eviction, as in 1 of 3 runs. E3 shows the same structure across
timeouts: slope 1.1 and an offset of 0.4 s. Nothing was skipped in any run.

**E4: sticky assignment moves fewer partitions but recovers no faster.** Time to full
throughput after the rebalance was under 1.5 s in every run of either arm. That is close to
the 1 s resolution of the metrics, and the arms overlap. With eager (round-robin)
assignment every survivor commits, gives up its partitions and resumes them from those
offsets. Nothing was processed twice in either arm (0 duplicates), and a consumer refills
its queue with one fetch. The prediction assumes losing an assignment is expensive, for
example rebuilding local state, but these consumers keep none. The stop-the-world
rebalance (6–8 s in both arms) dominates the outage.

**E5: fencing is what prevents the regression.** In every run the frozen zombie was
evicted after its 6 s session. Its partitions moved to the other consumer, which committed
well past the zombie's offsets during the freeze. In 5 of 6 runs the zombie, on waking,
committed its older offsets with its old ownership epoch. With fencing on, the coordinator
rejected all 6 such commits (`STALE_EPOCH`), and no committed offset moved backwards. With
fencing off, the same commits were accepted and moved both partitions back: for example
partition 2 from 3 207 to 2 446, which a restart would process again. In the sixth run the
zombie's overdue heartbeat reported the eviction before its commit was due, so it rejoined
without committing. Each fenced run had 21–35 duplicates: the zombie's work since its last
accepted commit, processed again by the new owner.

**E6: the write outage is longer than the election.** The new leader was always elected
from the ISR (broker 2, ISR {1, 2, 3}), and nothing acknowledged was lost. Producing
resumed only 14.2 s after the kill, against a 5.5 s election. The election only covers the
partitions the dead broker led. In the partitions where it was a follower it stays in the
ISR until it has not fetched for `replica.lag.time.max.ms` (10 s; the shrink came 12.4 s
after the kill in the first run), and a produce there commits only once every ISR member
has it. The producer stalls for every partition, not only the
dead leader's: its single sender thread waits for each batch's reply before sending the
next, so the other brokers' partitions wait behind the stalled one. A shorter
`replica.lag.time.max.ms` would shorten the gap, at the price of more ISR churn.

**E8: at-least-once duplication is exactly the uncommitted work.** Every crash was followed
by as many duplicates as the hook had processed since the last commit. Across 200 crashes
the duplicates added up to 15 532, the sum of those counts, and no record was skipped. The
means are within 10 % of (N − 1)/2. That is the spread expected from 50 uniform draws of k
per interval: the standard error is about 8 % of the mean.

**E9: the drain rate is the small difference of two large rates.** The survivors processed
331 ± 14 records/s each while draining, about 5 % above the 315/s calibrated with a
producer running, and between that and the 385/s measured draining a backlog with no
producer ([CALIBRATION.md](../experiments/CALIBRATION.md)). Their
spare capacity is 3 × 331 − 819 ≈ 174/s instead of 126/s, so a 5 % error in capacity
becomes a 40 % error in the drain. Using the measured capacity, 3 × capacity − rate
predicts each run's drain to within 15 % (208/212, 188/210, 129/140). The backlog was
small (about 1 300–1 600 records, under 2 s of production) and drained in under 10 s, so
each estimate rests on few samples.

## Deviations

- **E5 was run twice more.** In the campaign, compose started every service under an init
  (`init: true`), so `docker kill -s SIGSTOP` froze the init and not the consumer. The
  "zombie" never stopped and was never evicted, so neither arm exercised fencing. The fix
  (`674cdf2`) runs every binary as PID 1 again. The other experiments inject only
  `SIGKILL` (which takes down the whole container either way) or the crash hook, so their
  runs stand.
- **The first E5 rerun found a client bug (R-26).** In one control run, the consumer that
  took over the zombie's partitions re-read both from offset 0: 4 890 records already
  processed and committed. A sticky rebalance applied the new assignment before setting
  the added partitions' start offsets. In between, the still-running fetcher reset them to
  the earliest offset. Commits stayed correct, so no offset regressed. The fix (`eb40e37`)
  sets the start offsets in the same step as the assignment, and E5 was rerun again. The
  race shows up as extra duplicates. Every other experiment in the campaign had 0
  duplicates except E8, whose duplicates match the crash hook's counts exactly, so the
  race did not affect them. Both superseded E5 sweeps are kept in `runs/superseded/`,
  outside the archive.
- **E5's checks count only the stale commits the zombie actually sent** (`29ff072`). On
  waking, the zombie's overdue heartbeat and its overdue commit race. If the heartbeat
  reports the eviction first, the zombie drops its work and rejoins without committing,
  which is correct, and that run cannot test fencing. The checks were changed after the
  first rerun, which showed this.
- **E9's survivors' rate** was computed after the campaign with the corrected `plot_e9.py`
  (`6586605`), which leaves the bin before the drain out of the average. The drain rate
  and the check are unchanged.
- **Repeats:** 3 instead of the planned 5 (NFR-REPRO-04, cut line 3). E4's and E9's
  standard deviations are wide enough that more repeats would narrow them, but not change
  the verdicts.
